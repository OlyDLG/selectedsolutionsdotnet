## Section 1: Systems of Linear Equations: Substitution and Elimination

__42__, __46__, __50__, and __54__: Solve the given system or state that it is inconsistent if that is the case.

<a name="1.42" class="goback" onclick="winhisback()">__1.42__)</a>
$\begin{eqnarray} 
~~~~~2x & ~+ & ~~y &  &  & = & ~-4 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 1}\\
~~~~~ & ~- & 2y & + & 4z & = & ~~~~~~0 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 2}\\
~~~~~3x & & & - & 2z & = & -11  ~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 3}
\end{eqnarray}
$

__Sln__: There is no single &quot;correct&quot; way to solve these; in particular, experience, i.e., practice, will teach you &quot;intuition&quot; for a &quot;best&quot; approach for any given problem (if there is one: for arbitrary coefficients, i.e., in real life, one approach is unlikely to be more efficient than any other).&nbsp; Nevertheless, I will explain the approach that I take, because, in my experience, it will be the most efficient.

To begin with, the only variable it is &quot;easy&quot; to eliminate using substitution is $y$, because of its (understood) coefficient of $1$ in Eq. 1; but I can only use that substitution in Eq. 2, which would require me to multiply what $y$ equals by $-2$, and that’s more trouble than it’s worth, especially since in the next step I’d want to use elimination between that result and Eq. 3.&nbsp; Also, I see that if I multiply Eq. 3 by $2$ and add that to Eq. 2, I’ll eliminate $z$, and then I’ll be able to add that result to $2$ times Eq. 1 to eliminate $y,$ so I opt for that approach: 

$2(\text{Eq. 3}) + \text{Eq. 2} \implies (6x - 4z) + (-2y + 4z) = 6x - 2y = 2(-11) + 0 = -22$ (Eq. 4); next, $2(\text{Eq. 1}) + \text{Eq. 4} \implies (4x + 2y) + (6x - 2y) = 10x = 2(-4) + (-22) = -30 \implies \box{x = -3}$; substituting this into Eq. 1 yields $2(-3) + y = -6 + y = -4 \implies \box{y=2},$ and substituting this into Eq. 2 gives $-2(2) + 4z = -4 + 4z = 0 \implies \box{z=1}.$ We use Eq. 3 as a check: $3(-3) - 2(1) = -9 -2 = -11.~\chec$
<br><br>

<a name="1.46" class="goback" onclick="winhisback()">__1.46__)</a>
$\begin{eqnarray} 
~~~~~2x & ~- & 3y & - & z & = & ~0 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 1}\\
~~~~~ -x & ~+ & 2y & + & z & = & ~5 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 2}\\
~~~~~3x & - & 4y & - & z & = & ~1 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 3}
\end{eqnarray}
$

__Sln__: Since in this system all three equations have all three variables, I’m going to walk through my &quot;planning process&quot; again (but won’t after this problem: studying a problem to try to identify the most efficient approach is part of the skill set you should be developing here).

Adding Eq. 2 &quot;as is&quot; (i.e., without prior multiplication) to Eqs. 1 and 3 eliminates $z$, so that is our approach: Eq. 1 $+$ Eq. 2 gives $(2x - x) + (-3y + 2y) + (-z + z) = 0 + 5 \implies x - y = 5$ (Eq. 4); Eq. 2 $+$ Eq. 3 yields $(-x + 3x) + (2y - 4y) + (z - z) = 5 + 1 \implies 2x - 2y = 6 \implies x - y = 3$ (Eq. 5); subtracting Eq. 5 from Eq. 4 yields $(x - x) + [-y - (-y)] = 5 - 3 \implies 0 = 2$ which is untrue for any values of $x$ and $y.$ Thus this system is $\box{\text{inconsistent}}.$ Unfortunately, we don’t learn a method to check this &quot;non-existence&quot; of a unique solution till Section 3 on determinants, but I know that method and confirmed that this system is inconsistent. (The reader can try to check the system’s inconsistency using a three-D graphing calculator, such as [GeoGebra3D](https://www.geogebra.org/3d); I did that also and the three planes are such that it is not immediately obvious from their graphs that the three planes never intersect in a single point, i.e., a graphical check for this system is inconclusive.)
<br><br>

<a name="1.50" class="goback" onclick="winhisback()">__1.50__)</a>
$\begin{eqnarray} 
~~~~~3x & ~- & 2y & + & 2z & = ~~~6 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 1}\\
~~~~~7x & ~- & 3y & + & 2z & = -1 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 2}\\
~~~~~2x & - & 3y & + & 4z & = ~~~0 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 3}
\end{eqnarray}
$

__Sln__: Subtracting Eq. 2 from Eq. 1 yields $-4x + y = 7$ (Eq. 4); subtracting 2 times Eq. 1 from Eq. 3 gives $-4x + y = -12$ (Eq. 5); subtracting Eq. 5 from Eq. 4 yields $0 = 19$ which is $\box{\text{inconsistent}}.$ (Again, applying the the method we’ll learn in Section 3 verifies our result.)
<br><br>

<a name="1.54" class="goback" onclick="winhisback()">__1.54__)</a>
$\begin{eqnarray} 
~~~~~~x &~+ & 4y & - & 3z & = -8 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 1}\\
~~~~~3x & ~- & ~y & + & 3z & = \:12 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 2}\\
~~~~~~x &~+ & ~y & + & 6z & = ~~~1 ~~~~~~~~~~\text{Eq. 3}
\end{eqnarray}
$

__Sln__: Adding 4 times Eq. 2 to Eq. 1 gives $13x + 9z = 40$ (Eq. 4); adding Eq. 2 to Eq. 3 yields $4x + 9z = 13$ (Eq. 5); subtracting Eq. 5 from Eq. 4 yields $9x = 27 \implies \box{x = 3}$; substituting this into Eq. 5 gives $12 + 9z = 13 \implies \box{z = \Frac19}$; substituting these two results into Eq. 3 gives $3 + y + \Frac23 = 1 \implies \box{y = -\Frac{8}3}.$

Check: whenever I get fractional answers from a textbook problem, I’m extra-careful with my check step: Eq. 1 check: $3 + 4(-8/3) - 3(1/9) = (9 - 32 - 1)/3 = -24/3 = -8~\chec$; Eq. 2 check: $3(3) - (-8/3) + 3(1/9) = (27 + 8 + 1)/3 = 36/3 = 12~\chec$ (technically, I should also check the answer in Eq. 3, but since I used Eq. 3 to compute my last unknown, $y$, and my solution checks in the other two questions, I’m confident that I have obtained the correct answer.)
