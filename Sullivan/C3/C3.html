Section 2: The Graph of a Function

6) True or False  A function can have more than one \(y\)‑intercept.

Sln: \(\box{\text{False}}\): if it has more than one \(y\)‑intercept, then it intersects the \(y\)‑axis—a vertical line—more than once, and thus it fails the vertical line test.  (More formally, if it has more than one than \(y\)‑intercept, then \(x=0\) is being mapped to more than one value, which makes it not a function.)

16 and 22) Determine whether the graph is that of a function by using the vertical-line test.  If it is, use the graph to find: a) the domain and range; b) the intercepts, if any; and c) any symmetry with respect to the \(x\)‑axis, the \(y\)‑axis, or the origin.

16)

Sln: Any vertical line with equation \(x=a, -2 \lt a \lt 2\) intersects the graph in more than one point—one below the \(x\)-axis, one above, so \(\box{\text{not a function}.}~\) (Note that even though this is not a function, the intercepts and symmetries are still well-defined: the \(x\)-intercepts are \((\pm2,0)\), the \(y\)-intercepts are \((0,\pm2)\), and it is symmetric with respect to the origin and both axes.)

22)

Sln: Any vertical line intersects the graph only once, so it is the graph of a \(\box{\text{function}}.\) a) By convention, the absence of points indicating where the graph terminates implies that the graph continues on forever, in both directions horizontally and in the negative direction vertically; thus the \(\box{\text{domain is all real numbers }=\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty),}\) and the range is \(\box{(-\infty,5]}\) (all real numbers less than or equal to 5); b) it appears to have \(x\)-intercepts of \((-1,0)\) and \((2,0)\), and a \(y\)-intercept at or a little above \((0,4)\) (that’s the inherent difficulty in determining and citing values from graphs: they can never be precise); c) it is not symmetric with respect to the origin or either axis (it is symmetric with respect to the line \(x=0.5\), however).

26) Given \(f(x) = \Frac{x^2 + 2}{x + 4}\): a) Is \(\left(1,\Frac35\right)\) on the graph of \(f\)? b) What is \(f(0)\) and what is this point on the graph? c) Find \(x\) such that \(f(x) = \Frac12\) and state which point(s) this is (or these are) on the graph of \(f\). d) What is the domain of \(f\)? e & f) List the \(x\) and \(y\)-intercepts (if any).

Sln: a) A point \((x,y)\) is on the graph of \(y=f(x)\) if \((x,y)\) makes the equation \(y=f(x)\) a true statement; since \(f(1) = \Frac{1^2 + 2}{1 + 4} = \Frac35, \left(1,\Frac35\right)~\box{\text{is}}\) on the graph of \(f.\)

b) \(f(0) = \Frac{0^2 + 2}{0 + 4} = \Frac24 = \box{\Frac12}\), and thus the corresponding point on the graph is \(\box{\left(0,\Frac12\right)}.\)

c) We need \(x\) such that \(\displaystyle \frac{x^2 + 2}{x + 4} = \frac12\) and we’ve already found one such, \(\box{x=0}\); to find any others, multiply through by \(2(x+4)\) to yield \(2x^2+4=x+4 \implies 2x^2-x = 0 = x(2x-1) = 0 \implies 2x-1=0 \implies \box{x=\frac12}\) (check: \(\Frac{(1/2)^2+2}{1/2 + 4} = \Frac{9/4}{9/2}=\Frac12~\checkmark\)), which means the points \(\box{\left(0,\frac12\right)\text{ and }\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)}\) are on the graph of \(f.\)

d) The domain is all real numbers except any that make \(f\) undefined; in this case, the only thing that can make \(f\) undefined is division by zero, i.e., if \(x+4 = 0 \implies x=-4\), so this is the point we must exclude from the domain; thus the domian is \(\boxed{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}: x\ne -4\right\}.}\)

e & f) The \(y\)-intercept happens when \(x=0\), and we’ve already found that \(y\) value, so the \(y\)-intercept is \(\boxed{\left(0,\frac12\right)}\); the \(x\)-intercepts happen where \(y=0\), so we must solve \(\displaystyle \frac{x^2 + 2}{x + 4} = 0 \implies x^2+2=0\) which has no real solution, so the graph of this \(f\) has \(\boxed{\text{no }x\text{-intercepts.}}\)

30) Granny Shots The path of the ball in an underhand [basketball] foul shot, releasing the ball at a 70° angle from a position 3.5 feet above the floor, can be modeled by the function: \[h(x) = -\frac{136x^2}{\u^2} + 2.7x + 3.5\] where \(h\) is the height of the ball above the floor, \(x\) is the forward distance of the ball in front of the foul line, and \(\u\) is the initial speed of the ball in feet per second.

a) The center of the hoop is 10 feet above the floor and 15 feet in front of the foul line; determine the initial speed the ball must have in order for the ball to go through the hoop.

Sln: \(x_{final}=15, h_{final} = h(x_{final}) = h(15) = 10\), so we must solve \(-\Frac{136(15)^2}{\u^2} + 2.7(15) + 3.5 = 10\) for \(\u\): \(\implies 2.7(15) + 3.5 - 10 = 34 = \Frac{136(15^2)}{\u^2} \implies \u = 15\sqrt{\Frac{136}{34}} = 15\sqrt4 = \box{30\text{ fps}}\) (check: \(-\Frac{136(15^2)}{30^2} + 2.7(15) + 3.5 = -\Frac{136}4 + 40.5 + 3.5 = -34 + 44 = 10.~\checkmark\))

b) Write the function for the path of the ball using the velocity found in part a.

Sln: \(h(x; \u=30) = -\Frac{136x^2}{30^2} + 2.7x + 3.5 = -\Frac{\cancel4(34)}{\cancel{2^2}(15^2)}x^2 + 2.7x + 3.5 = \box{-\Frac{34}{225}x^2+2.7x+ 3.5}\)

c) Determine the height of the ball after it has traveled 9 feet in front of the foul line.

Sln: \(h(9; \u=30) = -\Frac{34}{225}(9^2)+2.7(9)+ 3.5 = -\Frac{34}{\cancel{3^2}(5^2)}(9^{\cancel2 1}) + 24.3 + 3.5 = \box{15.56\text{ ft}}\)

d) Find additional points and graph the path of the basketball.

Sln: At \(x=3\) ft, \(h(3; \u=30) = -\Frac{34}{\cancel{3^2}(5^2)}(\cancel{3^2})+2.7(3)+ 3.5 = \box{10.24\text{ ft}}\); \(h(6; \u=30) = -\Frac{34}{\cancel{3^2}(5^2)}(\cancel{3^2}2^2)+2.7(6)+ 3.5 = \box{14.26\text{ ft}}\)


34) Effect of Elevation on Weight If an object weighs \(m\) pounds at sea level, then its weight \(W\) (in pounds) at a height of \(h\) miles above sea level is given approximately by: \[W(h) = m\left(\frac{4000}{4000 + h}\right)^2\]

a) If Amy weighs 120 pounds at sea level, how much will she weigh [at the top of] Pike’s Peak, which is 14,110 feet above sea level?

Sln: Amy’s altitude is given in feet, whereas in \(W(h), h\) must be in miles, so we must first convert \(14110\cancel{\text{ ft}} \times \Frac{1\text{ mile}}{5280 \cancel{\text{ ft}}} = \Frac{1411}{528} \doteq 2.672\text{ mi}\); now we simply evaluate \(W\left(\Frac{1411}{528}\right) = 120\left(\Frac{4000}{4000+\Frac{1411}{528}}\right)^2 \doteq 120\left(\Frac{4000}{4000+2.672}\right)^2 \doteq 120(0.999332359)^2 \doteq 120(0.998665164) = \box{119.84\text{ lbs}}\)

b) Use a graphing utility to graph the function \(W = W(h)\); use \(m = 120\) pounds.

Sln:

c) Left to the reader.

d & e) At what height will Amy weigh 119.95 pounds?  Does your answer seem reasonable? Explain.

Sln: We need to solve \(119.95 = 120\left(\Frac{4000}{4000+h}\right)^2\) for \(h\); begin by dividing through by \(120\) to obtain \(\Frac{119.95}{120} = \Frac{23.99}{24} = \left(\Frac{4000}{4000+h}\right)^2\); next, take the square-root of both sides: \(\sqrt{\Frac{23.99}{24}} = \Frac{4000}{4000+h}\), and then reciprocate both sides: \(\sqrt{\Frac{24}{23.99}} = \Frac{4000+h}{4000} = 1 + \Frac h{4000}\); finally, subtract \(1\) from both sides: \(\sqrt{\Frac{24}{23.99}} - 1 = \Frac h{4000}\), and multiply by 4000: \[\box{h = 4000\left(\sqrt{\frac{24}{23.99}}-1\right) \doteq 0.834\text{ mi}}\] The graph in Part b tells us that weight goes down with increasing altitude; since 2.672 mi is greater than 0.834 mi, we should expect her decrease in weight to be not as great at the lower altitude, so \(\box{\text{yes}}\), this answer seems reasonable.

38) Is a graph that consists of a single point the graph of a function? Can you write the equation of such a function?

Sln: Yes: if the point has coordinates \((x,y)\), then \(f\) is the function whose domain is the singleton set \(\{x\}\); whose range is the singleton set \(\{y\}\); and whose formula—given this domain and range—is simply \(y=f(x).\)

46) Is there a function whose graph is symmetric with respect to the \(x\)‑axis? Explain.

Sln: No: in order for a graph to be symmetric wrt the \(x\)‑axis, for each \(x\) value mapped to a given \(y\) value, that same \(x\) value must also be mapped to \(-y,\) which means one \(x\) value is getting mapped to more than one \(y\) value.  This would violate the requirement that, for a relation to be a function, each domain value must be mapped to not more than one range value, so a graph symmetric wrt the \(x\)‑axis (and we have already seen such a graph in Problem 16 above) cannot be the graph of a function.

36, 40, and 44) Determine algebraically whether the given function is even, odd, or neither.

36) \(h(x) = 3x^3 + 5\)

Sln: \(h(-x) = 3(-x)^3 + 5 = -3x^3+5 \ne 3x^3+5\) (in general), so \(h\) is not even; nor does it equal \(-h(x) = -3x^3-5\) (in general), so it is not odd, so it is \(\boxed{\text{neither.}}\)

40) \(f(x) = \sqrt[\large3]{2x^2 + 1}\)

Sln: \(f(-x) = \sqrt[\large3]{2(-x)^2 + 1} = \sqrt[\large3]{2x^2 + 1} = f(x)\) for all \(x\), so \(f\) is \(\boxed{\text{even.}}~\) (Note: we don’t need to check for odd-ness because a function can’t be both even and odd; proof?)

44) \(\ds F(x) = \frac{2x}{|x|}\)

Sln: \(\ds F(-x) = \frac{2(-x)}{|-x|} = \frac{-2x}{|x|} = -\frac{2x}{|x|} = -F(x)\) for all \(x\) so \(F\) is \(\boxed{\text{odd.}}\)

46) Use the graph of \(f(x)\) below to find its absolute maximum and absolute minimum, if they exist.

Sln: The absolute maximum of a function, if there is one, is the function value, not necessarily unique, closest to "positive infinity" of all its relative maxima.  From the graph we see that \(f\) has relative maxima of \(2\) at \(x=0\) and \(4\) at \(x=4\); since \(4 \gt 2,\) the absolute maximum of of \(f\) is \(\box{4},\) which occurs at \(\box{x=4}\) (it is just a coincidence that the maximum value equals the \(x\)‑coordinate of where it occurs: generally, this will not be the case).  Similarly, the absolute minimum, if there is one, is the function value, not necessarily unique, closest to "negative infinity" of all the function’s relative minima.  Again, the graph tells us this \(f\) has relative minima of \(1\) at \(x=1\) (again, just a coincidence) and \(0\) at \(x=5\); since \(0 \lt 1\), \(\box 0\) is the absolute minimum, occurring at \(\box{x= 5.}\)

66) For \(f(x) = -4x + 1\), find: a) the average rate of change from \(x=2\) to \(x=5\); and b) an equation of the secant line containing \((2,f(2))\) and \((5,f(5))\).

Sln: a) By definition, the average rate of change of \(f\) from \(x=2\) to \(x=5\) is: \(\Frac{f(5)-f(2)}{5-2} = \Frac{-4(5)+1-(-4(2)+1)}3 = \Frac{-19-(-7)}3 =\) \(\Frac{-12}3 = \box{-4}\).

b) The secant line between two points of a line is simply the line itself (proof?), thus the required equation is simply \(\box{y=-4x+1}.\)

70) For \(h(x) = -2x^2 + x\), find: a) the average rate of change from \(x=0\) to \(x=3\); and b) an equation of the secant line containing \((0,h(0))\) and \((3,h(3))\).

Sln: For both parts, we need the values of \(h(0) = -2(0^2) + 0 = 0\) and \(h(3) = -2(3^2) + 3 = -15.~\) Therefore, the average rate of change (a) is \(\Frac{h(3)-h(0)}{3-0} = \Frac{-15}3 = \box{-5}\) and the equation of the secant line (b) is the equation of the line containing the points \((0,0)\) and \((3,-15).~\) Now, the equation of any (oblique) line containing the origin is of the form \(y=mx\) (why?), so we simply need to find \(m\) from the fact that \(-15=m(3)\) must be a true statement; this implies \(m=-5\), so the equation of the secant line is: \[\box{y = -5x}\]

12) Geometry A wire 10 meters long is to be cut into two pieces: one piece will be shaped as an equilateral triangle, and the other piece will be shaped as a circle.  a) Express the total area \(A\) enclosed by the pieces of wire as a function of the length \(x\) of a side of the equilateral triangle; b) what is the domain of \(A(x)\)? c) Graph \(A = A(x)\); for what value of \(x\) is \(A\) smallest?

a) The required area is the sum of the area of the triangle, \(\Frac{\sqrt3}4 x^2,\) and the area of the circle, \(\pi r^2\); \(r\) is determined using the fact that the circumference of a circle \(C = 2\pi r \implies r = \Frac C{2\pi},\) and after three lengths of \(x\) meters are used for the triangle, \(10 - 3x\) meters are left for the circumference of the circle, so \(r = \Frac{10-3x}{2\pi}.~\) Thus \[A(x) = \frac{\sqrt3}4 x^2 + \pi\left(\frac{10-3x}{2\pi}\right)^2 = \box{\frac{\pi\sqrt3 x^2 + 100-60x+9x^2}{4\pi} = \frac{(9+\pi\sqrt3)x^2 - 60x + 100}{4\pi}.}\]

26) Constructing an Open Box An open box with a square base is required to have a volume of 10 cubic feet: a) express the amount \(A\) of material used to make such a box as a function of the length \(x\) of a side of the square base; b) how much material is required for a base 1 foot by 1 foot; c) how much material is required for a base 2 feet by 2 feet? d) use a graphing utility to graph \(A = A(x)\); for what value of \(x\) is \(A\) smallest?

Sln: (Even though we consider it an important part of solving this problem, we leave drawing a picture of the situation to the reader.)

a) The volume \(V\) of a box with a square base of edge length \(x\) is \(V = x^2h\) where \(h\) is the height of the box, measured in the same units as the base edge length; given that \(V=10,\) \(h(x) = \Frac{10}{x^2}.~\) The box being open doesn’t affect the volume, but it does affect the area of material needed: we don’t need material for, presumably, the "top," i.e., the face opposite its square bottom, so the area of material required is \(A(x) = A_{\text{base}} + 4A_{\text{1 side}} = x^2 + 4xh =\) \[\box{x^2 + \frac{40}{x^2}.}\]
